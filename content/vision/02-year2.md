My vision is to design products that create genuinely connected experiences, hardware and software working together in ways that feel obvious to use, integrate into people's lives without friction, and stay useful at scale. This is the matured form of the networking-focused vision I wrote in year 1. The original phrasing was about networking technology democratizing access to the internet. The direction was right but the framing was narrow. What year 2 has shown me, through both formal coursework and real product work, is that the more interesting unit of design is the experience itself, not the connectivity that enables it. Connectivity matters because it lets devices, modules, and people behave as one system. The Design Connected Experiences elective was the first time I had a formal name for what I had already been gravitating toward.

The motivation has not changed in direction since year 1. I have wanted to build products at the intersection of technical functionality and aesthetic beauty since I first encountered Apple's design philosophy as a child, and that has steered every meaningful technical choice I've made. What has changed is the seriousness with which I now treat it. In year 1 I framed it as inspiration. In year 2 I am building it. I have a startup planned with a friend of mine and also a cofounder targeting modular peripherals, structured legally through an Estonian OÜ for registration by 31 August 2026. The first product is a modular keyboard system designed so each module is recognized at a glance, plugs in to populate an app for setup, and communicates its own interaction mode (touch, scroll, button) without instructions. Friends and coworkers are already asking weekly when they can have one. PRISM (student team), the landslide prediction system from the TU/e contest, is the test bed for the same vision applied to higher-stakes hardware: a mesh of small, self-sustainable devices with edge computing, deployed in remote terrain, cheaper than satellite imagery because the mesh network itself is the product.

The trends I am betting on are direct. Edge computing on inexpensive MCUs is pushing intelligence to the smallest devices in a network without dependence on cloud connectivity. Open-source silicon IP, particularly the RISC-V ecosystem, is moving custom MCU design from enterprise-only territory toward something a small hardware startup can credibly pursue across multiple product generations. Modular hardware that announces itself through software is no longer exotic, but it is rarely well-designed, and the gap between functional and intuitive is where Aesthetics of Interaction taught me the design discipline actually lives. The cross-disciplinary intersection of hardware engineering, software design, and user interaction is the space the embedded systems master degree I plan to enter will let me work in seriously.

On the societal side, my own operating context cuts across four EU jurisdictions: Belgian residency for cost, Dutch work and study, Romanian family, Estonian legal structure for the startup, with a Bulgarian cofounder. None of this is exotic on its own, but the practical experience of navigating it makes it unique. This has given me operational fluency for running an EU-wide business that other students don't get until they actually try. PRISM and the keyboard startup will both serve EU customers initially, and that knowledge is already in place.

The activities that bring this vision to reality run on three tracks across the next two years and beyond. The first is finishing the bachelor in year 3, including the retakes for Math and Data Analysis and pre-master electives selected specifically to bridge into the embedded systems master. The second is operating the startup: registering the Estonian OÜ by end of August 2026, building a v0 functional keyboard prototype with at least two working modules over the summer, running no-instruction usability tests on the friends and coworkers currently pushing me to ship, and iterating from there toward a first paying customer. The third is continuing PRISM through TU/e contest involvement, deepening both the hardware and the business-side work, since PRISM proves whether the connected-experience design philosophy holds in a higher-stakes context than peripherals. Past the bachelor, the embedded systems master is the bridge between what I can build now using off-the-shelf MCUs and what the startup needs in later product generations: custom hardware design using open-source silicon IP.

The deliberate choice to graduate in three years rather than take an exchange or internship is the one I have to defend most carefully. It would be easy to read as impatience. It is not. The professional and entrepreneurial contexts those programs are designed to provide are already running in parallel. The team lead role at Picnic is the operational experience an internship would offer. The cross-border life is an exchange in practice. The startup work is the founder experience neither program would deliver. An extra year of bachelor study would slow all three down for no tangible benefit. That is the trade I am making consciously.
